Fleece-lined knitwear is one of the few genuine product innovations in the sweater category in the past decade: the visual language of knitwear with the warmth performance of outerwear. It is also one of the easiest constructions to get wrong, because the lining changes the garment's engineering — its weight, its stretch behaviour, its wash performance and its cost structure.

For a brand, the important thing to understand is that a fleece-lined sweater is not a standard sweater with a layer added. It is a different construction with its own specification rules.

## What Fleece Lining Actually Is

The term covers several different things, and the differences matter commercially.

| Lining type | Construction | Character | Best suited to |
|------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Knitted fleece (brushed back) | Brushed inner surface on a knit fabric | Soft, breathable, moderate warmth | Light layering pieces, transition-season knits |
| Bonded micro-fleece | Separate fleece layer bonded to the knit | High warmth, structured body | Autumn/winter programmes, cold-climate markets |
| Fleece knit-in (double-face) | Two faces knitted together on the machine | Best stretch recovery, no delamination risk | Premium programmes, garments needing drape |

**Bonded and knit-in constructions are not equivalent**, and the difference shows up in the field. A bonded lining has an adhesive interface between two layers; if that bond is not properly engineered it can separate after repeated washing, which is a return-generating failure. A knit-in double-face construction eliminates that interface entirely but requires a more capable machine setup and a longer knitting time, so it costs more.

When a supplier quotes "fleece-lined," the useful question is which of these three it is — and then whether the lining is tested as part of the complete garment.

## Why the Specification Changes

Adding a lining alters the garment's engineering in ways that the base pattern does not account for.

**Weight and gauge.** A lining adds weight without adding visual bulk, so a fleece-lined sweater at the same gauge as a standard knit will be heavier. If your tech pack specifies GSM for the outer knit only, you have not specified the garment's actual weight, and the customer will feel a difference that the spec sheet does not explain.

**Measurement and stretch.** A bonded lining restricts stretch. A garment whose panels were graded for a single-layer knit will fit differently once a lining is bonded in — often snugger through the chest and shoulders. The size set must be produced in the actual lined construction, not modelled on the unlined version. This is one of the most common sources of fit complaints in lined knitwear.

**Care behaviour.** Two layers with different fibre compositions, shrinkage rates and moisture behaviour will respond to washing differently. A lining that shrinks more than the outer knit will pull the garment out of shape. This is why a wash test on the finished lined garment — not on the outer fabric alone — is essential.

## The Test Plan for a Lined Garment

The standard knitwear test set applies, with two additions that are specific to the construction:

| Test | Standard | Why it matters for a lined garment |
|------------|-------------|-------------|
| Dimensional change | ISO 6330 with ISO 5077 | Tests the lined garment as a unit, not the outer fabric |
| Pilling | ISO 12945-2 | Brushed surfaces and fleece are more prone to pilling |
| Colour fastness to washing | ISO 105-C06 | Checks for bleeding between lining and outer |
| Colour fastness to rubbing | ISO 105-X12 | Catches lining-to-outer colour transfer |
| Bond strength / delamination | Project-specific | Only applies to bonded constructions |
| Fibre composition | ISO 1833 series | Both layers must be declared separately on the label |

Two of these deserve emphasis because they are routinely skipped.

**Dimensional stability must be tested on the finished lined garment.** Testing the outer knit alone tells you nothing about how the assembly behaves, because the lining is a separate variable. If your test report describes a fabric rather than the garment, it does not cover the risk you are trying to manage.

**Composition labelling gets more complex.** If the garment has two distinct layers with different fibre content, the label must reflect what the customer is buying. A "70% wool / 30% nylon" claim on a sweater whose lining is polyester is a composition problem, not a marketing simplification, and it fails the same test a mislabelled single-layer sweater fails.

## Delamination: The Failure Mode to Design Out

For bonded constructions, delamination is the failure that generates returns, and it is preventable at specification stage.

What causes it: an inadequate or unevenly applied adhesive, insufficient bonding pressure or temperature, incompatible surfaces, or a construction that flexes more than the bond can tolerate. What it looks like in the field: bubbling or separation at the hem, cuffs and underarm — the areas of highest flex and moisture.

How to control it:

1. **Ask how the bond is made** — the adhesive type and the bonding process, not just the fact that it is bonded.
2. **Require a wash test on the bonded assembly**, repeated to your market's expectation, with the bond inspected afterwards.
3. **Ask about the flex zones specifically.** A bond that survives a flat panel test may still fail at a cuff.
4. **Consider knit-in double-face** if your programme cannot tolerate delamination risk at all, and price the trade-off honestly.

## Cost Drivers in Fleece-Lined Knitwear

A lined sweater costs more than an unlined one at the same gauge, and knowing why makes the quotation easier to challenge.

| Cost driver | Effect |
|------------|-------------|
| Lining material | An additional fabric layer, priced separately from the outer yarn |
| Bonding or second-face knitting | Additional machine time and process step |
| Added garment weight | Higher yarn and material consumption per piece |
| Greater thickness | More carton volume, so fewer pieces per container |
| Extended QC | Additional inspection points for bond and lining alignment |
| Longer production time | Lining adds a process stage to the critical path |

The freight consequence is easy to overlook. A lined sweater occupies more volume than an unlined one, so the pieces per carton and per container fall. On a volume-driven cargo, that changes your landed cost even when the factory price is unchanged. Build it into the cost model rather than discovering it at booking.

## Specifying a Lined Programme

A workable specification for a fleece-lined sweater includes:

- **Outer knit**: fibre composition, gauge, yarn count, stitch structure, fabric GSM
- **Lining**: fibre composition, construction type (bonded, brushed-back knit or knit-in double-face), weight
- **Combined garment weight**, not just outer fabric weight
- **Measured performance**: shrinkage limit, pilling rating and colour fastness on the finished lined garment
- **Bond specification** for bonded constructions, with a delamination test requirement
- **Label declaration** covering both layers where composition differs
- **Care instruction** derived from the lined garment's own wash test
- **Carton dimensions** reflecting the actual packed volume of the lined garment

Most of these are the same items you would specify for any sweater. The difference is that for a lined garment, the garment — not the fabric — is the unit of specification.

## FAQ

**Is a fleece-lined sweater warmer than a regular wool sweater?**
It depends on the construction and the layers. A bonded micro-fleece lining adds a genuine insulating layer and typically delivers more warmth than a single-layer knit of comparable outer fabric. A lightly brushed-back knit adds less. The reliable comparison is total garment weight plus lining type, not the marketing description.

**Can fleece-lined sweaters be machine washed?**
Only if the construction and the care test support it. Bonded constructions carry a delamination risk in machine washing, and two layers may shrink at different rates. The care instruction should come from a wash test performed on the finished lined garment, and knit-in double-face constructions generally perform better than bonded ones for machine-wash programmes.

**Why does a fleece-lined sweater fit differently from the same style unlined?**
Because the lining restricts stretch. Panels graded for a single-layer knit commonly feel snugger through the chest and shoulders once a lining is added. The size set must be produced and measured in the actual lined construction, which is why fit problems in lined knitwear usually trace back to approving an unlined sample.

**Does the fibre composition label need to declare the lining separately?**
The label must accurately describe the fibres in the garment, and where the outer knit and lining are different materials, both must be represented. A composition claim that describes only the outer layer while the lining is a different fibre is a labelling compliance problem in the US, EU and UK markets, and it is testable.

## The Bottom Line

A fleece-lined sweater is a different garment, not a sweater with something added. Specify the lining construction, test the finished garment rather than the outer fabric, design out delamination if you are using a bonded build, declare both layers on the label, and account for the extra carton volume in your landed cost. Brands that specify at this level get a product that performs as marketed; brands that specify only the outer knit get returns.
