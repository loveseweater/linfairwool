A sweater that arrives at a distribution centre in the wrong bag, with the wrong tag, folded the wrong way, is not a quality failure. It is a retail-readiness failure — and it costs the buyer money in rework, chargebacks or a slow start to the season.

Retail-ready packing is the discipline of specifying, at order stage, exactly how the garment should arrive so it can move from container to shop floor or fulfilment centre without being touched. It is one of the cheapest things to specify and one of the most expensive to get wrong, because packing errors are discovered in bulk.

## The Three-Layer Structure

A garment's packaging is a stack of decisions, each with its own purpose:

| Layer | Purpose | What to specify |
|------------|-------------|-------------|
| Polybag or tissue | Protect the garment in transit and handling | Material, thickness, recycled content, closure, venting |
| Hangtag and label set | Brand, price, size, barcode, compliance | Artwork, placement, attachment method, barcode type |
| Master carton | Protect and identify the shipment | Dimensions, board grade, count per carton, shipping marks |

The mistake to avoid is treating this as the factory's default. Factories pack to their own house standard unless told otherwise, and a house standard is optimised for the factory's handling, not for your retail channel. Specify each layer in writing.

## Polybags: What Retailers Actually Require

Polybag requirements are set by the retailer or marketplace, not by a single international standard, which is why they vary and why you should confirm them against your buyer's own packaging manual.

The requirements that come up most often:

- **Suffocation warning.** Major US retailers and Amazon require a suffocation warning on polybags with an opening of 5 inches (approximately 127 mm) or more. The conventional wording warns that the bag is not a toy and must be kept away from babies and children, and that it should not be used in cribs, beds, carriages or playpens. Confirm the exact wording with your buyer, as requirements are set by retailer policy and state law rather than one federal clause.
- **Thickness and clarity.** A minimum bag thickness of around 1.5 mil and a transparent bag are commonly specified, so barcodes can be scanned through the bag.
- **Venting.** Some buyers require perforated or vented bags to prevent condensation in transit, particularly for sea freight.
- **Recycled content.** Under the EU packaging regulation, plastic packaging will face recycled-content requirements from 2030 for certain formats, and buyers with sustainability commitments are asking for it earlier.

If your product ships to the EU, note what the packaging regulation does and does not say: it restricts specific single-use plastic formats such as grouped packaging and very lightweight carrier bags, but **it does not ban polybags on garments**. Polybags remain permitted and remain subject to recyclability, minimisation and labelling obligations.

## Barcodes and Identification

Retail identification runs on GS1 standards. GTINs — Global Trade Item Numbers — have four structures: 8, 12, 13 and 14 digits, all intended for global use.

For apparel, the two you will meet most are **GTIN-12**, which is synonymous with UPC and is the only GTIN type that can be encoded in a UPC-A barcode, and **GTIN-13**, synonymous with EAN and the only type encoded in an EAN-13 barcode. **GTIN-14** is used to identify trade item groupings such as a case or a set pack, and is not intended for point-of-sale scanning.

Two practical points follow. First, the barcode your factory applies and the barcode your retail customer expects must be the same structure — a UPC-A requirement with an EAN-13 tag is a rejected shipment. Second, the GTIN belongs to you, the brand, not to the factory, so you supply the numbers rather than receiving them.

Where barcodes sit matters too. A tag barcode that will be scanned through a polybag needs to be positioned and sized so it scans reliably, and if the tag is placed inside the fold of the garment it may be unreadable on arrival.

## Flat Pack or Hang Pack

This is a commercial decision disguised as a packing detail, and it changes your freight calculation.

**Flat pack** means the garment is folded and packed flat. It uses the carton volume efficiently, so you fit more pieces per carton and more per container. The trade-offs are fold lines and a handling step if the retail channel wants the garment presented on a hanger.

**Hang pack**, sometimes shipped as garments on hanger (GOH), presents the garment on a hanger, typically in a purpose-built garment container using a bar or string system. The advantages are reduced creasing and faster movement to the shop floor. The cost is space: hanging garments occupy considerably more container volume than flat-packed ones, so the effective pieces per container falls.

The decision usually follows the channel. Discount and mid-market programmes generally take flat pack; department store and premium programmes that need the garment on a hanger from arrival often specify GOH. Either way, ask for the carton dimensions and the pieces per carton before finalising your freight estimate.

## Carton Specifications and Shipping Marks

Carton content is the number that turns your order quantity into a shipment. Specify:

- **Carton dimensions and board grade**, so the cartons stack and survive handling
- **Pieces per carton**, by style, colour and size — with the breakdown, not just a total
- **Assortment convention**, meaning how sizes are distributed within a carton or a pre-pack
- **Shipping marks**, applied to the outer carton

Shipping marks are trade convention rather than a standardised content list, but a workable set covers: buyer or supplier identification, destination port, PO or order number, style number, colour, size range, quantity, carton number (for example 3 of 50), gross and net weight, carton dimensions and country of origin. Consistency matters more than the exact set — cartons that cannot be identified at the port slow down the whole shipment.

For packaging symbols, ISO 780 covers the graphical symbols for handling and storage of packages, and if your shipment is palletised, wooden pallets moving internationally must comply with ISPM 15, which requires heat treatment or fumigation and the IPPC mark.

## Filling the Container

Carton count becomes container selection. Published internal volumes give you the working figures:

| Container | Internal volume | Note |
|------------|-------------|-------------|
| 20GP | approximately 33.2 m³ | Suitable for smaller orders |
| 40GP | approximately 67.7 m³ | Standard volume option |
| 40HQ | approximately 76.3 m³ | Around 8-9 m³ more than a 40GP |

Two practical consequences for knitwear, which is a volume-driven cargo rather than a weight-driven one. First, the 40HQ adds roughly 31 cm of internal height over a 40GP, and because the freight difference between the two is usually small, the high-cube usually wins on cost per piece for light, bulky goods. Second, you never load to the theoretical volume — real loading efficiency is lower once carton sizes, palletisation and stacking limits are accounted for, so calculate your requirement from actual carton dimensions rather than from a rule of thumb in pieces.

There is also a compliance dimension to loading. Under the EU packaging regulation, from 2030 operators filling e-commerce or transport packaging must keep empty space below 50%, and filler materials count as empty space. For a knitwear programme, the practical response is to have the factory quote a right-sized carton rather than a standard one.

## Retail-Ready Is a Specification, Not a Request

The pattern across all of this is the same: the information has to be decided before production, written down, and confirmed on a sample. Labels printed before the fibre test, tags attached before the barcode is confirmed, cartons ordered before the dimensions are checked — each of these turns a cheap check into an expensive rework.

Ask for a **packing sample** — one finished garment, fully packed, photographed — before bulk packing begins. It is the cheapest control in the entire order.

## FAQ

**Do polybags on clothing require a suffocation warning?**
Major US retailers and Amazon require a suffocation warning on polybags with an opening of 5 inches (approximately 127 mm) or wider, and typically require the bag to be transparent and at least about 1.5 mil thick. Requirements are set by retailer policy and state law, so confirm the exact wording against your buyer's packaging manual.

**Does the EU ban polybags on garments?**
No. The EU packaging regulation restricts specific single-use plastic formats such as grouped packaging and very lightweight plastic carrier bags, but apparel polybags are not on the restricted list. Polybags remain subject to recyclability, recycled-content and labelling requirements that phase in from 2028.

**What is the difference between flat pack and hang pack?**
Flat pack folds the garment and packs it flat, fitting more pieces per carton and per container. Hang pack presents the garment on a hanger, usually in a garment container, which reduces creasing and speeds up retail handling but uses significantly more container volume.

**What should a master carton shipping mark include?**
A typical set covers buyer and supplier identification, destination port, order number, style, colour, size range, quantity, carton number, gross and net weight, carton dimensions and country of origin. There is no single mandatory standard — the requirement is that cartons can be identified consistently throughout the shipment.

## The Bottom Line

Retail-ready packing is decided in the tech pack, not on the packing desk. Specify the polybag material and warning, confirm the barcode structure your retail customer scans, choose flat pack or hang pack against the channel rather than habit, size the carton properly, and approve a packing sample before bulk. None of these steps is expensive. Skipping any of them is.
